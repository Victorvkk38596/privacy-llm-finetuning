\section{Project Overview \& Foundation Papers}

\subsection{Problem Statement and Research Objective}

Language models are frequently fine-tuned on sensitive internal data, such as medical
histories, confidential legal documents, or personal correspondence. While adaptation
specializes the model for a target task, it introduces the risk of training data leakage.
An adversary querying the fine-tuned model can execute a \textbf{Membership Inference Attack (MIA)}
to infer whether a specific target record was included in the fine-tuning split.

\textbf{Differential Privacy (DP)} addresses this risk by bounding individual record
influence during training. In DP-SGD, per-sample gradients are clipped to a maximum norm $C$
and perturbed with calibrated Gaussian noise proportional to $\sigma$. The privacy guarantee
is parameterized by $(\varepsilon, \delta)$, where lower $\varepsilon$ corresponds to stronger
theoretical protection. However, applying DP-SGD to large models typically causes significant
accuracy loss, as stochastic gradient perturbation accumulates across all updated parameters.

This project investigates the mechanistic relationship between layer position and noise tolerance:

\begin{center}
\emph{``How does the depth of fine-tuned transformer layers (early, middle, or late) govern task utility and empirical privacy leakage under DP-SGD when the formal privacy budget is held constant?''}
\end{center}

Our primary hypothesis is that \textbf{late layers} (nearer to the output) capture more
task-specific and class-discriminative semantics than early layers. Consequently, late layers
should provide a stronger gradient signal per parameter, yielding higher task utility for the
same perturbation scale. We also investigate whether an automated, perturbation-aware layer
selection algorithm can match or exceed intuitive contiguous layer selections.

Finally, we audit all models using shadow-model likelihood ratio attacks to determine whether
updating different depths or parameter fractions leads to measurable variations in empirical
privacy leakage.

\subsection{Foundation Papers and Synthesis}

\textbf{Paper 1: DP-SelFT (Sha et al., arXiv:2605.17432, 2025).}
Sha et al.\ introduce \emph{Differentially Private Selective Fine-Tuning} (DP-SelFT). The
method observes that different transformer layers exhibit varying resilience to gradient noise.
The selection pipeline works in three stages: (1) generate a task-aligned proxy candidate pool
with a small privacy allocation $\varepsilon_{\text{sel}}$; (2) evaluate candidate layer
subsets on this proxy data under worst-case gradient perturbations matching the scale of
downstream DP noise; and (3) fine-tune only the selected layer subset on private data with
DP-AdamW using the remaining budget $\varepsilon_{\text{ft}} = \varepsilon - \varepsilon_{\text{sel}}$.
On standard benchmarks, DP-SelFT reports improvements over full DP fine-tuning by restricting
noise accumulation to a subset of parameters.

\textbf{Paper 2: Marek et al. (arXiv:2606.09401, ICLR 2026).}
Marek et al.\ provide a rigorous benchmark of \emph{empirical privacy leakage} in DP fine-tuned
language models using offline \textbf{Robust Membership Inference Attacks (RMIA)}. The paper
demonstrates that a formal $\varepsilon$ guarantee does not alone describe empirical vulnerability;
distributional similarity between pretraining and fine-tuning data plays a primary role. The
authors establish that evaluating RMIA requires an in-distribution \textbf{shadow reference
model} trained on a disjoint split of the same dataset. Without a calibrated shadow model,
reference-based attacks lose their discriminative power under DP.

\textbf{Synthesis and Project Scope.}
Our study operates at the intersection of both works. Paper~1 supplies the layer selection
and perturbation ranking framework, while Paper~2 provides the empirical audit protocol. We
evaluate Paper~1's selective configurations using Paper~2's \textbf{symmetric swap-pair
shadow protocol}. Specifically, we train target model $M_A$ on split $D_A$ and reference model
$M_B$ on split $D_B$, allowing each model to serve as the other's shadow reference. This setup
yields bidirectional empirical MIA metrics across layer positions and parameter fractions under
identical privacy budgets.

\section{Achievements to Date}

\subsection{Infrastructure Built}

We built an end-to-end experimental framework to ensure reproducible training and auditing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Data pipeline (\texttt{src/data.py}): parses GLUE SST-2, constructs a single-parent
        containment forest to link sentence fragments to root sentences, and enforces Policy~F1
        (at most one phrase per family per partition) across seeded splits.
  \item Training engine (\texttt{src/train.py}): step-based DP-SGD loop using Opacus, RDP
        privacy accounting, \texttt{BatchMemoryManager} for memory-bounded per-sample clipping,
        gradient telemetry logging, and multi-split logit extraction.
  \item Model management (\texttt{src/models.py}): supports selective parameter freezing by
        layer index, block grouping, or classification head, while strictly freezing embedding
        weights to protect memory and privacy accounting.
  \item Layer tournament (\texttt{src/select\_layers.py}): implements DP-SelFT perturbation-aware
        layer ranking over candidate blocks, writing selections to configuration files.
  \item Audit engine (\texttt{src/audit.py}): computes naive loss baselines, continuous ratio
        RMIA, and offline Zarifzadeh probability-ratio formulations, calculating ROC-AUC,
        TPR at 1\% and 5\% FPR, and generalization gaps.
  \item Orchestrator (\texttt{run\_experiment.py}): manages execution of the 8-configuration
        grid under the symmetric swap-pair protocol with automated metric aggregation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experiments Completed}

Two full grid sweeps were executed on Kaggle GPU hardware (RoBERTa-base, GLUE SST-2, $N{=}1024$,
$\delta{=}10^{-5}$). In the initial sweep (v1), all DP configurations collapsed to 50.0\%
accuracy because the unaligned classification head received high gradient noise ($\sigma \approx 2.06$).
In the revised sweep (v2), we introduced a 100-step clean head warmup and differential learning
rates before engaging DP-SGD, resolving the collapse. Results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results}.

\subsection{Key Research Findings}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Layer depth governs DP utility at constant parameter count.} All three-layer
        configurations update exactly 17.5\% of model parameters, yet accuracy rises from 72.2\%
        (early blocks 0--2) to 84.8\% (late blocks 9--11). This represents a 12.6 percentage point
        gain attributable entirely to layer position.
  \item \textbf{Selective late tuning outperforms full DP fine-tuning.} Tuning only layers 9--11
        achieves 84.8\% accuracy, exceeding full DP fine-tuning (81.0\%) by 3.8 percentage points
        despite updating $3.9\times$ fewer parameters (17.5\% vs.\ 68.7\%). This confirms that
        high-dimensional gradient noise ($\propto \sqrt{d_\Lambda}\sigma C$) degrades representation
        quality across lower layers when the full backbone is updated.
  \item \textbf{Contiguous late layers match or exceed algorithmic selection.} The DP-SelFT
        selection algorithm identified blocks (3, 6, 4) and reached 82.9\% accuracy. However,
        contiguous late layers (Control $H'$, blocks 9--11) reached 84.7\% under the exact same
        $\varepsilon = 4.7$ budget, showing that simple proximity to the output head provides
        stronger representation adaptation than scattered block selection.
  \item \textbf{Formal DP bounds empirical leakage across all layers.} All DP configurations
        produced RMIA AUC values between 0.49 and 0.53, indicating that membership leakage remains
        near chance regardless of parameter fraction or layer position.
  \item \textbf{Head-only fine-tuning creates an informational bottleneck.} Updating only the
        classification head (0.48\% of parameters) reached only 58.0\% accuracy, demonstrating
        that frozen backbone representations cannot separate sentiment classes without at least
        partial transformer block adaptation.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip
\textbf{Summary.}
The core training pipeline, layer tournament, and swap-pair auditing framework are operational.
Our primary midterm finding demonstrates that selective fine-tuning of late transformer layers
(blocks 9--11) under DP-SGD achieves 84.8\% validation accuracy, outperforming full DP
fine-tuning (81.0\%) while keeping membership leakage near chance (RMIA AUC 0.51). The remaining
work will map the complete privacy-utility Pareto frontier across multiple seeds, test tighter
privacy budgets ($\varepsilon \le 1.0$), and evaluate the $k$-fraction scaling curve.
