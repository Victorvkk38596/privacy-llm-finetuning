\section{Project Overview \& Foundation Papers}

\subsection{Problem Statement and Research Objective}

Language models are frequently fine-tuned on sensitive internal data, such as medical
histories, confidential legal documents, or personal correspondence. While adaptation
specializes the model for a target task, it introduces the risk of training data leakage.
An adversary querying the fine-tuned model can execute a \textbf{Membership Inference Attack (MIA)}
to infer whether a specific target record was included in the fine-tuning split.

\textbf{Differential Privacy (DP)} addresses this risk by bounding individual record
influence during training. In DP-SGD, per-sample gradients are clipped to a maximum norm $C$
and perturbed with calibrated Gaussian noise proportional to $\sigma$. The privacy guarantee
is parameterized by $(\varepsilon, \delta)$, where lower $\varepsilon$ corresponds to stronger
theoretical protection. However, applying DP-SGD to large models typically causes significant
accuracy loss, as stochastic gradient perturbation accumulates across all updated parameters.

This project investigates the mechanistic relationship between layer position and noise tolerance:

\begin{center}
\emph{``How does the depth of fine-tuned transformer layers (early, middle, or late) govern task utility and empirical privacy leakage under DP-SGD when the formal privacy budget is held constant?''}
\end{center}

Our primary hypothesis is that \textbf{late layers} (nearer to the output) capture more
task-specific and class-discriminative semantics than early layers. Consequently, late layers
should provide a stronger gradient signal per parameter, yielding higher task utility for the
same perturbation scale. We also investigate whether an automated, perturbation-aware layer
selection algorithm can match or exceed intuitive contiguous layer selections.

Finally, we audit all models using shadow-model likelihood ratio attacks to determine whether
updating different depths or parameter fractions leads to measurable variations in empirical
privacy leakage.

\subsection{Foundation Papers and Synthesis}

\textbf{Paper 1: DP-SelFT (Sha et al., arXiv:2605.17432, 2025).}
Sha et al.\ introduce \emph{Differentially Private Selective Fine-Tuning} (DP-SelFT). The
method observes that different transformer layers exhibit varying resilience to gradient noise.
The selection pipeline works in three stages: (1) generate a task-aligned proxy candidate pool
with a small privacy allocation $\varepsilon_{\text{sel}}$; (2) evaluate candidate layer
subsets on this proxy data under worst-case gradient perturbations matching the scale of
downstream DP noise; and (3) fine-tune only the selected layer subset on private data with
DP-AdamW using the remaining budget $\varepsilon_{\text{ft}} = \varepsilon - \varepsilon_{\text{sel}}$.
On standard benchmarks, DP-SelFT reports improvements over full DP fine-tuning by restricting
noise accumulation to a subset of parameters.

\textbf{Paper 2: Marek et al. (arXiv:2606.09401, ICLR 2026).}
Marek et al.\ provide a rigorous benchmark of \emph{empirical privacy leakage} in DP fine-tuned
language models using offline \textbf{Robust Membership Inference Attacks (RMIA)}. The paper
demonstrates that a formal $\varepsilon$ guarantee does not alone describe empirical vulnerability;
distributional similarity between pretraining and fine-tuning data plays a primary role. The
authors establish that evaluating RMIA requires an in-distribution \textbf{shadow reference
model} trained on a disjoint split of the same dataset. Without a calibrated shadow model,
reference-based attacks lose their discriminative power under DP.

\textbf{Synthesis and Project Scope.}
Our study operates at the intersection of both works. Paper~1 supplies the layer selection
and perturbation ranking framework, while Paper~2 provides the empirical audit protocol. We
evaluate Paper~1's selective configurations using Paper~2's \textbf{symmetric swap-pair
shadow protocol}. Specifically, we train target model $M_A$ on split $D_A$ and reference model
$M_B$ on split $D_B$, allowing each model to serve as the other's shadow reference. This setup
yields bidirectional empirical MIA metrics across layer positions and parameter fractions under
identical privacy budgets.

\section{Midterm Achievements and Pilot Results}

\subsection{Infrastructure Built}

We built an end-to-end experimental framework to ensure reproducible training and auditing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Data pipeline (\texttt{src/data.py}): parses GLUE SST-2, constructs a single-parent
        containment forest to link sentence fragments to root sentences, and enforces Policy~F1
        (at most one phrase per family per partition) across seeded splits.
  \item Training engine (\texttt{src/train.py}): step-based DP-SGD loop using Opacus, RDP
        privacy accounting, \texttt{BatchMemoryManager} for memory-bounded per-sample clipping,
        gradient telemetry logging, and multi-split logit extraction.
  \item Model management (\texttt{src/models.py}): supports selective parameter freezing by
        layer index, block grouping, or classification head, while strictly freezing embedding
        weights to protect memory and privacy accounting.
  \item Layer tournament (\texttt{src/select\_layers.py}): implements DP-SelFT perturbation-aware
        layer ranking over candidate blocks, writing selections to configuration files.
  \item Audit engine (\texttt{src/audit.py}): computes naive loss baselines, continuous ratio
        RMIA, and offline Zarifzadeh probability-ratio formulations, calculating ROC-AUC,
        TPR at 1\% and 5\% FPR, and generalization gaps.
  \item Orchestrator (\texttt{run\_experiment.py}): manages execution of the 8-configuration
        grid under the symmetric swap-pair protocol with automated metric aggregation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pilot Experiments (Midterm)}

Two full grid sweeps were executed on Kaggle GPU hardware (RoBERTa-base, GLUE SST-2, $N{=}1024$,
$\delta{=}10^{-5}$). In the initial sweep (v1), all DP configurations collapsed to 50.0\%
accuracy because the unaligned classification head received high gradient noise ($\sigma \approx 2.06$).
In the revised sweep (v2), we introduced a 100-step clean head warmup and differential learning
rates before engaging DP-SGD, resolving the collapse. Results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results}.

\subsection{Pilot Observations (single seed; superseded by Section~\ref{sec:corrected})}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Layer depth governs DP utility at constant parameter count.} All three-layer
        configurations update exactly 17.5\% of model parameters, yet accuracy rises from 72.2\%
        (early blocks 0--2) to 84.8\% (late blocks 9--11), a 12.6 percentage point gap in this
        single-seed pilot. Whether the gap repeats across seeds without the private warmup is what
        the corrected grid tests.
  \item \textbf{Selective late tuning outperforms full DP fine-tuning.} Tuning only layers 9--11
        achieves 84.8\% accuracy, exceeding full DP fine-tuning (81.0\%) by 3.8 percentage points
        despite updating $3.9\times$ fewer parameters (17.5\% vs.\ 68.7\%). This is consistent with
        the noise-dimension argument (total noise $\propto \sqrt{d_\Lambda}\sigma C$), but one seed does
        not establish it.
  \item \textbf{Contiguous late layers match or exceed algorithmic selection.} The DP-SelFT
        selection algorithm identified blocks (3, 6, 4) and reached 82.9\% accuracy. However,
        contiguous late layers (Control $H'$, blocks 9--11) reached 84.7\% under the exact same
        $\varepsilon = 4.7$ budget in this pilot. The selection step's proxy labels were copied from
        the nearest private $D_{\text{tune}}$ example without noise, so $\varepsilon_{\text{sel}} = 0.3$
        does not cover it; automatic selection therefore remains a pilot result only.
  \item \textbf{Near-chance RMIA AUC for all DP pilot runs.} All DP configurations produced RMIA
        AUC values between 0.49 and 0.53. This describes the evaluated offline RMIA attack (one
        reference model) under this setup; it is an empirical measurement, not a consequence of the
        formal guarantee, which in any case did not cover the v2 warmup stage.
  \item \textbf{Head-only fine-tuning creates an informational bottleneck.} Updating only the
        classification head (0.48\% of parameters) reached only 58.0\% accuracy (one of the two models
        stayed at the majority class), suggesting that partial block adaptation matters under DP.
\end{itemize}

\section{Second Half: Corrected Method and Core Grid}\label{sec:corrected}

\subsection{Correction to the classifier warmup}

\textbf{What changed.} The pilot v2 pipeline ran 100 non-private AdamW steps on the classifier head
using batches from the private training split, and only then called \texttt{make\_private}. That
stage has been removed. \texttt{src/train.py} now accepts \texttt{head\_warmup\_source} $\in$
\{\texttt{none}, \texttt{public\_imdb}\}; the default is \texttt{none}, and any other value (including a
private-split warmup) raises an error. The optional public warmup uses a seeded, class-balanced sample of
4{,}096 reviews from IMDb (\texttt{stanfordnlp/imdb}, train split, IMDb's own review-level labels), the
same 100 steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, and the same data for every DP configuration within a seed.

\textbf{Enforced ordering.} The private training split is wrapped in a ledger
(\texttt{src/privacy\_guard.py}). In a DP run, reading any private example before the Opacus
accountant is attached raises an error. The one library-internal exception is Opacus'
\texttt{DPDataLoader} constructor, which reads \texttt{dataset[0]} to learn tensor shapes for empty Poisson
batches; these reads are logged separately and the ledger checks that no optimizer step occurs during them.
After training, each model's record stores: the event index at which accounting started, the index of the
first private read, the accountant's step count at that read (must be 0), and whether the accountant's
total step count equals the number of logical optimizer steps.

\textbf{Smoke test and step-1 decision.} \texttt{scripts/run\_core\_grid.py --stage smoke} runs a 3-step
late-3 DP swap pair and checks those conditions, and also checks that a private-split warmup is rejected.
The step-1 gate then trains early-3, late-3 and all-12 at $\varepsilon = 5$, seed 42, with no warmup.
Rule fixed before any corrected run: a model has \emph{collapsed} if validation accuracy $< 55\%$ or it
predicts the positive class for fewer than 5\% or more than 95\% of validation examples (majority-class
accuracy on SST-2 validation is 50.92\%). If any gate model collapses, the gate is repeated with the
public IMDb warmup, and whichever variant is chosen is applied to every DP configuration. The
no-warmup runs stay in the records either way.

\subsection{Core grid design}

Seeds 42, 43 and 44. Each (configuration, seed) is one two-model swap pair ($M_A$ on $D_A$ audited with
$M_B$ as reference, and vice versa). Within a seed the partition is deterministic, so every configuration
uses the same $D_A$, $D_B$, $D_{\text{out}}$ and $D_{\text{pop}}$; the partition hash is saved in each record and
the analysis refuses to run if two configurations of one seed disagree. All DP configurations share one
recipe: AdamW, backbone LR $10^{-3}$, head LR $3\times10^{-3}$, weight decay 0, linear schedule with 5\% LR
warmup, 300 steps, logical batch 64, physical batch 16, $C = 1$, $\delta = 10^{-5}$, embeddings frozen.
The clean baseline keeps its existing recipe (LR $3\times10^{-5}$, 1000 steps) and is shown for utility
context only.

\subsection{Analysis procedure}

For each (configuration, seed) the two swap-pair models are averaged. Configurations are summarised by
the mean and sample standard deviation of the three seed-level values, with each seed's value shown.
Comparisons use matched seeds: for each seed, the difference to the reference arm (late-3, $\varepsilon{=}5$)
is computed, and we report its mean, SD and the number of seeds sharing its sign. A 95\% $t$-interval is also
saved, but with three seeds it has two degrees of freedom ($t = 4.30$), so its half-width is about
$2.5\times$ the SD of the differences, and it reflects seed-to-seed variation (partition, initialisation, DP
noise and data order together), not uncertainty about the population of all possible runs. We call an
accuracy difference \emph{repeated} only if it has the same sign in all three matched seeds, and make no
significance claims. RMIA AUC near 0.5 is reported as the result of this offline RMIA attack with one
reference model, not as evidence about the formal guarantee.

\subsection{Limitations and remaining work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pending runs.} Smoke test, step-1 gate, validation runs and the three-seed grid
        (\texttt{RESULTS\_PENDING.md}). Runtime estimates will use measured wall time from the first
        completed runs (\texttt{aggregate\_grid.py} with \texttt{--estimate}); no measured runtime
        exists yet.
  \item \textbf{Hyperparameters were chosen with private data} (pilot runs and the reporting validation set),
        which the accountant does not cover.
  \item \textbf{Three seeds} give only a rough view of variability; intervals are wide.
  \item \textbf{One audit.} Offline RMIA with a single reference model is a fairly weak attacker;
        near-chance AUC does not rule out leakage to stronger attacks (more reference models, canaries).
  \item \textbf{Recipe fixed across budgets.} The same 300-step recipe is used at $\varepsilon = 1$, where
        $\sigma \approx 4.5$; collapse there would describe this recipe, not DP at $\varepsilon = 1$ in general.
  \item \textbf{Clean baseline} uses a different recipe (1000 steps, LR $3\times10^{-5}$) and is context only.
  \item \textbf{Middle-3} is the repository's existing definition (blocks 4--6), which is not exactly centred.
  \item \textbf{Deferred to a separate pass:} automatic layer selection (pilot only, see Table~\ref{tab:coverage}),
        LoRA, canary extraction and a second dataset.
\end{itemize}
